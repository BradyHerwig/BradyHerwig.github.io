Case study 01 · CSCN 421

Vehicle complaint topics

Which defect themes show up in 30,000 NHTSA consumer complaints from 2025, and do those themes shift by census region? I compared a 15-topic LDA model with a structural topic model and kept LDA.

What it shows

Defects, then region

City and state were cleaned and joined to a census file. Exact matches came first. Rows that missed got a same-state fuzzy match, kept only when the edit distance was 2 or less. 1,866 complaints stayed Unmatched. Negation words were joined to the next word, so "not good" became not_good. Topic shares are percents inside each region. The South has the most complaints, so raw counts would follow that volume. Dealer service and warranty was about 11% in the Northeast, South, and West. Fuel-pump complaints were 11.3% in the Midwest and 5.8% in the West.

What I learned

Scores are not the topic

Ten topics merged brakes with steering. Twenty topics added a brand-name topic and merged seats with windshields. Fifteen kept the parts apart. STM looked better on exclusivity and sat farther from its neighbors, and it was slower. I kept LDA because a complaint theme that names a part is the useful output. Grok proposed short labels from the top terms. I kept a label only when those terms supported it.

Case study 02 · CSCN 421

Telecom churn models

Five models on a customer churn table: KNIME and scikit-learn KNN, KNIME and scikit-learn SVM, and a soft-voting ensemble. The search metric was recall of Yes. A customer who churns after we predicted they would stay is the miss that matters.

70/30

Stratified split. Transforms fit on train, applied to test.

Yes recall

Grid-search score on the training folds

~80%

Accuracy on most models. Always-No is about 73%.

0.819–0.844

ROC-AUC across the five models

What it shows

Same split, five models

CustomerID came out. Missing values, one-hot encoding, and scaling were fit on the training rows and then applied to the test rows. Only the three float columns were normalized. KNIME KNN settled at k = 13 after the gains past that were small. The Python KNN search picked 15 neighbors and uniform weights. KNIME SVM used an RBF kernel, C = 1, sigma = 10. KNIME KNN and the Python SVM tied for the fewest false negatives.

What I learned

Pick the metric first

Accuracy sat a little above the majority class and hid the false negatives. Scoring the grid search on recall of Yes made the search match the cost. The ensemble used soft voting because KNN and an RBF SVM miss different customers, and two voters need probabilities rather than a hard majority.

Case study 03 · CSCN 421

CIFAR-10 CNNs

Can a small convolutional network clear 65% on CIFAR-10, and what actually moves that number? I changed one thing at a time, then retrained the winner with augmentation, then compared both with MobileNetV2.

65.4%

Test accuracy, 3-conv CNN, no augmentation

71.8%

Same CNN with flips, 15° rotation, color jitter

87.1%

MobileNetV2, ImageNet weights, head replaced

~100 min

Transfer learning on CPU after the GPU limit

Slide 1 of 3

What it shows

One change at a time

CIFAR-10 is 60,000 color images, 32 by 32, in 10 classes. I split the 50,000 training images 80/20 with the torch seed set to 42, and left the 10,000-image test set alone until the end. A third convolution, batch norm, learning rate 0.001, and batch size 64 beat a two-layer start. Dropout and a larger batch both did a little worse. Augmentation used the same architecture and added about six points for almost the same 550 seconds.

What I learned

Pretrained features cost time

MobileNetV2 started from ImageNet weights. I froze the feature extractor, replaced the classifier with a linear layer of 10 outputs, and resized inputs to 160 with ImageNet normalization. Three epochs reached 87.1% on the test set. The last run was on CPU after Colab's GPU limit, about 100 minutes. The custom nets stayed near 9 minutes. The accuracy gap is the pretrained features. The time gap is the forward pass through a much larger net.

Case study 04

NamUs Missing Persons Analysis

How do demographic and geographic characteristics of NamUs missing-person listings differ between juveniles and adults—and which factors best distinguish the two groups when modeled with logistic regression versus a decision tree?

Classification EDA Scikit-learn Public data

Slide 1 of 4

What it shows

Patterns in public listings

End-to-end analysis of public NamUs missing-person listings: clean the extract, explore juvenile vs adult profiles, then classify age_group from sex, state, and race/ethnicity only—never age, which would leak the label. Logistic regression led on overall accuracy; the tree recovered more juveniles. Results describe patterns in a listing database, not true risk of going missing.

What I learned

Metrics that matter

Accuracy alone can hide minority-class failure. Comparing an additive logistic model to a tree makes the cost of ignoring interactions concrete. Practiced leakage-aware features, stratified evaluation, and careful framing of sensitive public data.
