Case study 01 · Advanced Machine Learning

Vehicle complaint topics

In 2025, 30,000 people wrote complaints to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration about vehicle problems. I grouped those write-ups into themes and checked whether the themes differ by region of the country. I compared two grouping methods and kept the one whose themes named separate car parts.

Written complaints from 2025

15

LDA

Method I kept. Its themes named separate car parts.

Midwest complaints about fuel pumps. The West was 5.8%.

Decision Kept. Topics named separate parts. Scored higher on distance, mixed the defects.

What it shows

Where the complaints came from

I matched each complaint's city and state to a U.S. region. Close spellings counted only in the same state. Fuel-pump complaints were 11.3% of Midwest complaints and 5.8% in the West.

What I learned

A higher score was not a clearer theme

Fifteen themes kept brakes, steering, seats, and windshields apart. Ten and twenty themes merged some of those parts. I kept the 15-theme model because its themes named separate car parts.

Case study 02 · Advanced Machine Learning

Which customers cancel

The question is which phone customers will cancel. I trained five models on the same table, using both KNIME, a visual workflow tool, and scikit-learn in Python. I scored them on how many cancellations they caught. Missing a customer who leaves costs more than flagging someone who stays.

70/30

70% used to train, 30% held out to test, with the same share of cancellations in both

Catch rate

The search tried to catch cancellations, not to maximize overall accuracy

~80%

Rows labeled correctly. Guessing "stays" every time would be about 73%.

0.819 to 0.844

How well each model ranks customers who leave above customers who stay. 1.00 would be perfect.

What it shows

Same split, five models

I removed the customer ID and prepared the columns from the training rows only, then applied those steps to the test rows. The KNIME nearest-neighbor model and the Python support-vector model tied for the fewest missed cancellations.

What I learned

Overall accuracy hid the misses

Most customers stay, so a model can look about 80% right and still miss many cancellations. I scored the search on how many cancellations it caught.

Case study 03 · Advanced Machine Learning

Recognizing small photos

CIFAR-10 is a practice set of 60,000 small color photos in 10 categories, such as cars, birds, and ships. I wanted a small image network to get at least 65% of a held-out test set right, then I changed one thing at a time to see what raised that number.

65.4%

Share of test photos correct, small network, original photos only

71.8%

Same network, trained on flipped, slightly rotated, and recolored photos

87.1%

MobileNetV2, already trained on a large photo set, last layer replaced

~100 min

That last run, on a regular processor after the free GPU time ran out

What it shows

One change at a time

I left 10,000 photos untouched until the design was chosen. Training on flipped and recolored copies of the same photos raised accuracy by about six points.

What I learned

A network that had already seen photos

MobileNetV2 had already learned from a large set of everyday photos. I trained only its last layer, and 87.1% of the test photos were correct. That run took about 100 minutes.

Case study 04

Missing-person listings

NamUs is a public database of missing and unidentified people. I compared juvenile listings with adult listings, then tried to tell the two groups apart from sex, state, and race or ethnicity. I used logistic regression and a decision tree.

Classification Public data Scikit-learn

What it shows

Patterns in public listings

I predicted juvenile or adult from sex, state, and race or ethnicity, and I left age out because that was the answer. Logistic regression was right more often overall. The decision tree found more of the juvenile cases.

What I learned

A high overall score can miss a smaller group

Most listings are adults, so a high overall score can still miss juveniles. These are patterns in a public database, not a measure of someone's risk of going missing.
